A Practical Guide to Shannon Entropy for Stock Market Analysis: From Information Theory to Trading Signals
Introduction: Information, Uncertainty, and the Modern Market
The central and enduring challenge in quantitative finance is the measurement, interpretation, and management of uncertainty. Financial markets, particularly stock markets, are complex adaptive systems characterized by a multitude of interacting agents, feedback loops, and a constant influx of new information. The resulting price movements often follow a "jagged path," appearing random and unpredictable. This apparent randomness is not merely noise; it is a fundamental characteristic of an efficient competitive market, where participants actively seek and exploit any discernible patterns, thereby eliminating them in the process. Traditional financial models have long sought to tame this uncertainty, but often do so by relying on restrictive and simplifying assumptions, such as the normality of asset return distributions. Empirical evidence, however, consistently demonstrates that financial returns are not Gaussian; they exhibit properties like heavy tails (leptokurtosis) and volatility clustering, meaning extreme events are more common and periods of high and low volatility tend to persist, respectively. These stylized facts challenge the applicability of models built on a foundation of normality and highlight the need for more robust, non-parametric tools.   

A new paradigm for quantifying uncertainty emerged from a field seemingly distant from finance: communications engineering. In his monumental 1948 paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," Claude E. Shannon established the discipline of information theory, providing for the first time a rigorous, quantitative framework for understanding communication as a statistical process. Shannon's work was revolutionary not just for its engineering applications—which laid the groundwork for the entire digital age—but for its profound philosophical insight: information itself could be measured. He introduced the concept of entropy as a universal measure of the uncertainty, choice, or average information content inherent in a system's state. This framework proved to be applicable far beyond its original context, finding utility in fields as diverse as thermodynamics, computer science, neurobiology, and, eventually, economics and finance.   

This report argues that Shannon Entropy, the cornerstone of information theory, offers a powerful, non-parametric, and model-free lens for analyzing stock market dynamics. It provides a means to quantify the degree of randomness or predictability in price movements, serving as a direct test of market efficiency, a robust alternative to traditional volatility-based risk metrics, and a novel guide for portfolio construction and diversification. However, the true utility of entropy in finance is unlocked only through a methodologically rigorous application that acknowledges and corrects for the unique statistical properties of financial time series. A naive calculation can be misleading; a carefully considered one can reveal deep insights into market structure and behavior. This guide provides the essential theoretical foundations, a detailed practical workflow for its calculation, and a critical survey of its primary applications, drawing upon decades of research from the interdisciplinary fields of econophysics and quantitative finance.

Here, f(x) is the probability density function (PDF) of the continuous random variable X.   

While analogous in form, differential entropy has several properties that make it a problematic and non-intuitive extension of its discrete counterpart.

It can be negative: Unlike Shannon entropy, which is always non-negative, differential entropy can take on negative values. For example, the differential entropy of a uniform distribution over the interval [0,1/2] is −log(2), which is negative for any base greater than 1.

It is not a limit of discrete entropy: One might expect that as the bin size of a discretized continuous variable approaches zero, the Shannon entropy would converge to the differential entropy. This is not the case; the Shannon entropy diverges to infinity. Differential entropy differs from this limit by an infinite offset, making it a relative, rather than absolute, measure of uncertainty.   

It is not invariant to coordinate changes: A simple linear scaling of a variable changes its differential entropy, which is a highly undesirable property for a fundamental measure of information.

These theoretical difficulties are the primary reason why, in virtually all practical applications of entropy to financial time series, the first and most critical step is the discretization of the continuous data. This process converts the continuous return series into a finite set of symbols or states, allowing for the direct and robust application of the original Shannon entropy formula. The challenge then shifts from a theoretical one to a practical one: how to perform this discretization in a meaningful way.

The conceptual lineage of entropy, from its roots in thermodynamics to its formalization in information theory, provides a powerful lens for financial analysis. The term "entropy" was first introduced in thermodynamics by Rudolf Clausius and later given a statistical mechanical interpretation by Ludwig Boltzmann to describe the degree of disorder or the number of microscopic arrangements available to a physical system. The second law of thermodynamics famously states that in an isolated system, entropy can only increase or stay the same, meaning the system naturally evolves towards a state of maximum disorder or thermal equilibrium.   

Shannon's crucial insight was to recognize that the mathematical form of Boltzmann's entropy formula could be generalized and applied to any system governed by probabilities, thereby abstracting it from its physical origins. This conceptual leap gave birth to the field of    

econophysics, which applies principles and methods from statistical physics to understand economic and financial phenomena, treating markets as complex dynamical systems. However, the analogy is not perfect and its limitations are as instructive as its successes. Financial markets are fundamentally different from the isolated systems of classical thermodynamics. They are open systems, constantly being perturbed by external interventions such as the arrival of new information, central bank policy changes, government regulations, and the collective actions of traders.   

This distinction is what makes entropy a dynamic and insightful measure for markets. While a closed physical system might passively drift towards a state of maximum entropy (randomness), the entropy level of a financial market is an active reflection of how its participants are processing information and interacting. A sudden and sharp decrease in market entropy, therefore, is not a violation of physical law but a powerful signal. It signifies a phase transition in the market's collective state, moving from a regime of diverse, independent, and heterogeneous decision-making to one of correlated, synchronized, herding behavior. This is often triggered by a single, overwhelmingly powerful piece of public information, such as the news of a financial collapse or a global pandemic, which aligns investor actions and makes the market temporarily more "ordered" and, paradoxically, more predictable. Thus, entropy evolves from being a static measure of complexity into a dynamic indicator of market regime, collective psychology, and informational efficiency.

Methodologies for Defining Bins/Symbols: To overcome the limitations of fixed thresholds, more robust methods are used to define the boundaries of the discrete states:

Fixed-Width Binning: The range of returns (from min to max) is divided into k equally sized bins. This method is simple but highly sensitive to outliers, which can create very wide bins, and can result in some bins having very few observations while others are densely populated.

Quantile-Based Binning: The data is divided into k bins such that each bin contains an equal number of observations. For example, using quartiles (k=4) or deciles (k=10). This approach is far more robust to the underlying distribution of returns and is a common choice in empirical research. Symbolic Time Series Analysis (STSA): This advanced technique moves beyond single-period states to capture temporal patterns. Instead of calculating the entropy of individual symbols, it calculates the entropy of sequences of symbols, or "words." For example, with a binary alphabet ('U', 'D'), one could analyze patterns of length 2. This creates a new state space of four possible "words": 'UU', 'UD', 'DU', and 'DD'. The probabilities of these words are then calculated from their frequencies in the time series. This method can capture short-term dependencies like momentum ('UU', 'DD') or mean-reversion ('UD', 'DU') and provides a richer measure of the system's complexity.   

The choice of discretization method involves a trade-off. Simpler methods like binary symbolization are easy to implement and interpret but may lose significant information. More complex methods like STSA can capture more intricate dynamics but require more data for reliable probability estimation and can be sensitive to the choice of "word" length.

2.3 The Filtering Imperative: Isolating "Genuine" Inefficiency
A naive calculation of Shannon entropy on a raw or even a discretized return series can be profoundly misleading, particularly when the goal is to measure market efficiency. The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) posits that asset prices fully reflect all available information, implying that future price changes are unpredictable based on past information. In information-theoretic terms, an efficient market should behave like a martingale process, exhibiting maximum randomness and therefore maximum entropy. A calculated entropy value that is significantly lower than this theoretical maximum is thus interpreted as a sign of predictability, and therefore, market inefficiency.   

However, this interpretation is complicated by the fact that financial time series contain several well-documented statistical regularities that reduce randomness but are not necessarily indicative of exploitable arbitrage opportunities. These patterns, if not accounted for, will artificially lower the calculated entropy, leading to a false detection of market inefficiency. The most prominent of these regularities are:

Intraday Seasonality: In high-frequency data, volatility and trading volume often follow a predictable U-shaped or J-shaped pattern throughout the trading day, with higher activity near the open and close.

Volatility Clustering: Periods of high volatility tend to be followed by more high volatility, and periods of low volatility by more low volatility. This phenomenon, also known as heteroskedasticity, is famously modeled by GARCH-family models.

Microstructure Noise (for HFT): At the highest frequencies (tick-by-tick or minute-by-minute data), effects like the bid-ask bounce (prices oscillating between the bid and ask prices) and price staleness (periods of zero returns due to infrequent trading) introduce spurious patterns and correlations.   

Because these patterns are well-known and already incorporated into the expectations of sophisticated market participants, they do not represent a "free lunch." An entropy measure that is low simply because it is capturing the predictable nature of volatility clustering is not measuring a market failure, but rather one of the market's most famous stylized facts. To use entropy as a true measure of genuine market inefficiency—that is, predictability that is not explained by these known effects—one must first "whiten" or filter the return series to remove these regularities. The entropy is then calculated on the residuals of this filtering process. This transforms the application of entropy from a simple calculation into a sophisticated, multi-step signal processing task. Advanced academic research, particularly in the high-frequency domain, emphasizes this filtering as an essential prerequisite for meaningful analysis.   

A state-of-the-art filtering methodology, as proposed by researchers like Shternshis, Mazzarisi, & Marmi, involves the following sequential steps :   

Remove Intraday Seasonality: The raw high-frequency returns are first deseasonalized. This is typically done by dividing the return at a specific time interval by the average standard deviation observed for that same interval across many days.

Remove Volatility Clustering: A GARCH(1,1) model (or a similar variant) is fitted to the deseasonalized returns. The model captures the time-varying volatility. The standardized residuals from this model, which should ideally be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with constant variance, are then extracted.

Remove Microstructure Noise: An Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA) model is fitted to the standardized GARCH residuals. This step removes any remaining short-term linear autocorrelation caused by market microstructure effects.

The Shannon entropy calculated on the final filtered residuals of this multi-step process provides a "genuine" measure of the market's informational efficiency, stripped of its most common and non-exploitable patterns.

Part III: Applications and Seminal Research in Financial Analysis
The application of Shannon entropy to finance has spawned a rich and diverse body of research over several decades, moving from a theoretical curiosity to a practical tool in the quantitative analyst's arsenal. Its primary uses cluster around four key areas: assessing market efficiency, measuring risk, optimizing portfolios, and identifying advanced trading signals.

3.1 The Entropy-Efficiency Nexus
The most direct and foundational application of entropy in finance is as a tool to test the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH). The EMH, in its weak form, asserts that all past trading information is already reflected in current asset prices, making future price movements unpredictable based on historical data. This implies that price returns should follow a random walk, a process characterized by maximum disorder. Shannon entropy provides a natural and quantitative measure of this disorder. High entropy values, approaching the theoretical maximum for a given discretization, are consistent with market efficiency, while low entropy values suggest the presence of patterns, predictability, and thus, inefficiency. A key advantage of entropy is its non-parametric nature; unlike many classical statistical tests (e.g., autocorrelation tests), it does not require assumptions about the stationarity of the time series or a specific underlying probability distribution, making it well-suited for the complex realities of financial data.   

Empirical studies have consistently leveraged this connection. Early work by Maasoumi and Racine demonstrated that entropy-based measures could successfully detect nonlinear dependencies and predictability in stock returns that were invisible to traditional linear models. More recent and methodologically advanced studies, such as those by Shternshis, Mazzarisi, and Marmi, have applied entropy to high-frequency data. After rigorously filtering out known regularities like volatility clustering and microstructure noise, they found a weak but statistically significant signal of inefficiency even in highly liquid markets like ETFs, suggesting that fleeting predictive opportunities may exist at very short timescales.   

Perhaps the most compelling and consistently observed finding relates to the behavior of entropy during financial crises. Numerous studies covering events from the 1987 crash to the 2008 Global Financial Crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic have found that market entropy decreases significantly during periods of systemic stress and sharp market declines. This counter-intuitive result—that markets become more "orderly" during a crisis—is attributed to a shift in investor behavior. In normal times, a diversity of opinions, strategies, and information sources leads to high-entropy, random-like price movements. During a crisis, however, fear and panic create a powerful, unifying signal that leads to    

herding behavior. As a vast number of market participants sell indiscriminately, their actions become highly correlated, reducing the diversity of market outcomes and making the system's behavior more predictable (i.e., downward trending). This implies that market efficiency is not static; it dynamically shifts from strong-form during calm periods to weak-form during crises, a phenomenon that entropy is uniquely suited to capture.   

3.2 Entropy as a Superior Risk Metric
Beyond market efficiency, entropy has been proposed as a more robust and comprehensive measure of financial risk compared to the industry standard, standard deviation (volatility). The primary critique of standard deviation is its reliance on the second moment of the distribution and its sensitivity to extreme, outlier events. It implicitly assumes that the risk is adequately captured by the dispersion around the mean, which is only truly appropriate for normal or near-normal distributions. Shannon entropy, by contrast, considers the    

entire probability distribution. It is a function of all higher-order moments and captures information about the shape, peakedness, and asymmetry of the distribution, offering a more holistic measure of uncertainty.   

Comparative analyses with established volatility models like the GARCH family yield nuanced results. GARCH models are parametric and explicitly designed to capture the stylized fact of volatility clustering. Entropy, being model-free, provides an independent assessment of uncertainty. Some studies suggest that entropy and GARCH-based volatility capture different facets of risk. For instance, Ghosh and Nisha (2020) found that entropy may be more accurate at measuring periods of frequent but low-magnitude volatility, while GARCH models excel at capturing the impact of large, infrequent shocks. Other research has shown that the information-theoretic results from entropy do not always align with GARCH findings, particularly during crises, suggesting they are complementary rather than interchangeable tools.   

Entropy has also been linked to established tail-risk measures like Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES). VaR estimates the maximum potential loss over a specific time horizon at a given confidence level, while ES measures the average loss in the tail of the distribution beyond the VaR level. Empirical work by Pele et al. (2017) uncovered a significant negative relationship between the entropy of intraday returns and these intraday risk measures. They found that periods of lower entropy (higher predictability) were associated with higher VaR and ES. This suggests that the increased orderliness that makes markets more predictable during downturns is also associated with a higher probability of extreme losses. This relationship can be exploited to build more accurate forecasting models for VaR and ES, using entropy as a predictive variable.

This approach directly addresses one of the major practical failings of MVO: its tendency to produce highly concentrated and unstable portfolios that are extremely sensitive to small errors in the estimation of expected returns and covariances.   

Recent developments have focused on integrating entropy into the classical framework, leading to Mean-Variance-Entropy (MVE) models. These are multi-objective optimization models that simultaneously aim to:

Maximize expected portfolio return.

Minimize portfolio variance (risk).

Maximize the entropy of portfolio weights (diversification).

By adding entropy as a third objective, these models penalize over-concentration and generate portfolios that are more robust, better diversified, and often exhibit superior out-of-sample performance compared to their MVO counterparts.   

3.4 Advanced Frontiers: From Trading Signals to Information Flow
The most practical and direct application of entropy for algorithmic trading lies in its use as a regime filter or a dynamic signal conditioner. The core insight is that markets are not uniformly predictable; they transition between states of high and low entropy. Trading strategies can be designed to exploit these transitions.   

Identifying Low-Entropy Regimes: A quantitative trader can continuously monitor the entropy of a market or a specific trading signal. A significant drop in entropy signals a temporary increase in order and predictability, creating a "window of opportunity." For example, a mean-reversion strategy, which performs poorly in random-walk markets, could be programmed to activate only when entropy falls below a certain threshold and to deactivate when it reverts to its mean.   

Continuous Entropy Monitoring and Risk Management: Conversely, a sudden spike in entropy can serve as a "circuit breaker." It indicates that the market has become more random and that any existing informational edge has likely weakened. This signal can be used to automatically reduce position sizes or even exit the market entirely to manage risk in real-time.   

A more advanced concept from information theory that is gaining traction in finance is Transfer Entropy (TE). Introduced by Thomas Schreiber in 2000, TE is a non-parametric measure of directed information flow between two time series. It quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about the future state of a variable    

X that is gained from knowing the past states of another variable Y, over and above the information already provided by the past of X itself. Unlike simple correlation, TE can detect nonlinear relationships and is inherently directional (i.e., TE

Part IV: A Critical Perspective: Limitations and Future Directions
While Shannon entropy provides a versatile and powerful framework for financial analysis, it is not without significant limitations and conceptual challenges. A critical understanding of its weaknesses is essential for its proper application and for appreciating its role as a complementary tool, rather than a replacement for, traditional financial and economic analysis.

4.1 Methodological Challenges and Sensitivities
The practical implementation of entropy analysis is fraught with methodological choices that can heavily influence the results.

Parameter Dependence: The final entropy value is highly sensitive to the parameters chosen during the calculation process. Key parameters include the lookback window used to estimate probabilities, the number of bins or quantiles used for discretization, and the length of "words" in Symbolic Time Series Analysis (STSA). There is no universally optimal set of parameters; they must be chosen based on the specific characteristics of the data (e.g., frequency) and the research question at hand. This subjectivity can make results difficult to compare across studies.   

Data Requirements and Non-Stationarity: The accurate estimation of a probability distribution, which is the input to the entropy formula, requires a substantial amount of data. This presents a fundamental dilemma in finance. Financial markets are notoriously non-stationary, meaning their statistical properties (like mean, variance, and correlation structures) change over time due to evolving economic conditions, technological shifts, and changes in investor behavior. To obtain statistically reliable probability estimates, a long lookback period is desirable. However, a long window may average over multiple distinct market regimes, obscuring the very dynamics one wishes to study. A short window is more adaptive to change but may suffer from noisy and unreliable probability estimates.   

The "Scalar Reduction" Problem: A significant theoretical limitation is that Shannon entropy reduces an entire, potentially complex, probability distribution to a single number. This is a form of information loss. It is possible for two distributions with very different shapes and risk implications—for example, a bimodal distribution (suggesting two distinct market states) and a flat, uniform distribution—to have the exact same entropy value. The single scalar value of entropy obscures these crucial underlying characteristics, failing to capture intricate dependencies and potentially decoupling the measure from other important statistical properties like variance or skewness.

4.2 Conceptual Critiques: Econophysics and Market Realities
The intellectual foundation for applying entropy to finance lies in the field of econophysics, which draws analogies between financial markets and complex physical systems. While this approach has yielded valuable insights, it has also faced trenchant criticism from mainstream economists for what they see as a reductive and overly simplistic view of market dynamics. These critiques highlight the conceptual limits of the information-theoretic approach.   

The core of the critique is that physical analogies, such as modeling traders as interacting "particles" in a gas, fundamentally misunderstand the nature of economic agents and markets. Unlike particles, which are governed by universal and time-invariant physical laws, human market participants are intentional, strategic, and adaptive. They learn from experience, change their behavior, and do not interact randomly. Their decisions are driven by a complex interplay of rational calculation, psychological biases, and institutional constraints, none of which are captured in a simple particle model.   

Furthermore, a central feature of real-world financial markets is asymmetric information: the condition where some participants possess more or better information than others. This information imbalance is a primary driver of trading and price discovery, and it leads to well-known market phenomena like adverse selection (e.g., sellers of "lemon" stocks knowing more than buyers) and moral hazard. Standard entropy calculations, which are based on a single, system-wide probability distribution, implicitly assume that all information is public and that all agents are drawing from the same probabilistic model. They cannot account for the strategic behavior that arises from information asymmetry.   

This points to a deeper, more fundamental limitation. Shannon's original theory was explicitly designed to solve the technical problem of communication: how to reproduce a message selected at one point, at another point, with maximum efficiency and accuracy. The theory is deliberately agnostic about the    

semantic content or meaning of the message being transmitted. In finance, however, meaning, context, and interpretation are paramount. A corporate earnings report (the "message") is not just a string of bits; its impact on the market depends entirely on how it is interpreted in light of prior expectations, macroeconomic conditions, and prevailing investor sentiment. Shannon entropy can expertly measure the statistical complexity and predictability of the    

price signal that results from this process, but it is fundamentally blind to the underlying economic, strategic, and psychological drivers that generate that signal. It can tell you how unpredictable the market is, but it cannot tell you why. Therefore, entropy-based analysis should be viewed as a valuable complement to, not a substitute for, traditional economic and behavioral theories of finance.

4.3 The Future of Entropy in Finance
Despite its limitations, the application of entropy in finance is an active and evolving field of research, with several promising future directions.

Integration with Machine Learning: Entropy is increasingly being used not as a standalone predictive tool, but as a powerful feature or meta-parameter within more complex machine learning (ML) models. For instance, an ML model could be trained to use market entropy as an input to determine which other features (e.g., technical indicators, fundamental ratios) are likely to be most predictive in the current market regime. Entropy can also be used for feature selection or as a regularization term in a model's loss function to encourage certain properties in the output.   

Generalized Entropies: Research is actively exploring the use of generalized entropy formulations that can offer a more nuanced view of uncertainty. The two most prominent are Rényi entropy and Tsallis entropy. Both are controlled by a parameter, typically denoted q or α, which allows them to emphasize different parts of the probability distribution. For values of q<1, these entropies are more sensitive to rare events (the tails of the distribution), while for q>1, they are more sensitive to the most likely outcomes (the mode of the distribution). Studies have suggested that Tsallis entropy, for example, may be a more effective indicator during short, sudden market crashes, where tail events dominate.   

High-Frequency Trading (HFT): The domain of HFT is perhaps the most natural fit for advanced entropy analysis. At microsecond timescales, market dynamics are dominated by microstructure effects, and the rigorous, filtering-based methodologies for calculating "genuine" entropy are not just an academic exercise but a practical necessity. Identifying fleeting, statistically significant deviations from randomness is the core challenge of HFT, and entropy-based tests provide a theoretically sound basis for developing such strategies.   

Conclusion: Entropy as an Indispensable Tool in the Quantitative Analyst's Toolkit
Shannon Entropy, a concept born from the need to quantify the limits of communication, has proven to be a remarkably versatile and insightful tool for deconstructing the complexity of financial markets. It is not a panacea, nor does it offer a simple key to unlock market prediction. Its true strength lies in its non-parametric nature, providing a rigorous, model-free language to measure uncertainty, randomness, and information content without the restrictive assumptions that underpin many classical econometric models. It offers a unified framework for analyzing phenomena as seemingly disparate as the efficiency of high-frequency markets, the collective psychology of a financial crisis, and the diversification of an investment portfolio.

The primary takeaway of this guide is that the practical value of entropy is directly proportional to the methodological rigor of its application. The journey from a raw time series of stock prices to a meaningful and interpretable entropy value is a multi-stage process that demands careful consideration. Robust methods for calculating returns, thoughtful strategies for discretization, and, for advanced applications, a sophisticated filtering of known statistical regularities are not optional refinements but essential prerequisites for avoiding spurious conclusions. A naive calculation measures noise; a rigorous one measures information.

In an era increasingly defined by an overwhelming deluge of data and the ascendancy of algorithmic trading, the fundamental concepts that Shannon laid out more than seventy years ago are more critical than ever. The ability to quantify information, to measure uncertainty, and to distinguish genuine predictability from statistical artifact is the core task of the modern quantitative analyst. When applied with the computational power and methodological sophistication of the 21st century, Shannon's theory remains a profoundly relevant and indispensable part of the analyst's toolkit, offering insights that are orthogonal, complementary, and often deeper than those provided by traditional financial analysis alone.

Appendix: Annotated Bibliography of Seminal and Recent Research
Shannon, C. E. (1948). A Mathematical Theory of Communication. Bell System Technical Journal, 27(3), 379-423.
This is the foundational paper of information theory. Shannon introduces the concepts of self-information and entropy as mathematical measures of uncertainty and information content. He develops the source coding theorem, proving that entropy represents the fundamental limit of lossless data compression, and lays the groundwork for the entire digital communication era. Its application to other fields, including finance, stems directly from the universal statistical framework established in this work.

Philippatos, G. C., & Wilson, C. J. (1972). Entropy, market risk, and the selection of efficient portfolios. Applied Economics, 4(3), 209-220.
This is the seminal paper that first introduced the concept of entropy into the field of portfolio selection. The authors proposed a mean-entropy optimization model as an alternative to Markowitz's mean-variance framework. Their key innovation was to use the Shannon entropy of the portfolio's weight distribution as a direct, quantitative measure of diversification, arguing it was a more general measure of uncertainty than variance. They found that the resulting mean-entropy efficient portfolios were consistent with those produced by traditional models.

Maasoumi, E., & Racine, J. (2002). Entropy and predictability of stock market returns. Journal of Econometrics, 107(1-2), 291-312.
This influential paper demonstrated the power of entropy as a non-parametric tool for detecting predictability in financial time series. The authors used an entropy-based metric to measure dependence between stock returns. Their key finding was that this metric could capture significant nonlinear relationships and predictability that were completely missed by traditional linear models like autocorrelation tests, providing strong evidence against the weak-form efficient market hypothesis.

Risso, W. A. (2008). The informational efficiency and the financial crashes. The ICFAI University Journal of Business Strategy, 5(4), 58-71.
This paper is representative of a body of work that uses entropy to analyze market behavior during crises. Risso's key finding is that market efficiency, as measured by entropy, is not static and tends to decrease significantly during market crashes. This decrease in entropy corresponds to an increase in order and predictability, often due to herding behavior, which provides a quantitative basis for understanding why markets may become more forecastable during periods of extreme stress.

Schreiber, T. (2000). Measuring Information Transfer. Physical Review Letters, 85(2), 461-464.
This paper introduced the concept of Transfer Entropy, a sophisticated and powerful extension of Shannon's original work. Schreiber defined transfer entropy as a non-parametric measure for detecting the directionality of information flow between two systems. It has become a crucial tool in econophysics for mapping causal networks in financial markets, allowing researchers to investigate, for example, whether information flows from one stock index to another or from news sentiment to price returns.

Shternshis, A., Mazzarisi, P., & Marmi, S. (2022). Measuring market efficiency: The Shannon entropy of high-frequency financial time series. Chaos, Solitons & Fractals, 162, 112403.
This recent paper represents the state-of-the-art in applying entropy to test market efficiency in high-frequency data. The authors' primary contribution is the development and application of a rigorous, multi-step filtering methodology designed to remove known, non-exploitable regularities (like seasonality, volatility clustering, and microstructure noise) from the data before calculating entropy. By computing the entropy of the filtered residuals, they isolate a "genuine" measure of inefficiency, finding that while the ETF market is not perfectly efficient at a one-minute scale, the signal of inefficiency is weak once known patterns are accounted for.
